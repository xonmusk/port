From: IT Security Team <it-security@jprnogan-alerts.com>

Subject: ⚠️ URGENT: Account Verification Required — Immediate Action Needed

Our security monitoring systems have detected unauthorized access attempts on your corporate account. As part of our mandatory security protocol, you must verify your credentials within the next 2 hours or your account will be temporarily suspended.

Please click the link below: https://jpmorgan-secure-verify.accounts-center.com/auth

Do not share this email or forward it to colleagues

1
Spoofed sender domain. The email comes from "jprnogan-alerts.com" — not the real jpmorgan.com. Attackers register domains that look almost identical at a glance. Always check the actual email address, not just the display name.
2
Urgency & alarm language. "URGENT," "Immediate Action Needed" — this emotional pressure is designed to bypass your rational thinking. Legitimate IT communications rarely use alarm-style subject lines.
3
Artificial time pressure. "Within the next 2 hours" creates panic. Real security protocols don't impose arbitrary 2-hour deadlines via email. This is a classic pressure tactic to prevent you from thinking critically or consulting others.
4
Suspicious URL. "accounts-center.com" is not a JPMorgan domain. The URL uses "jpmorgan" as a subdomain prefix to appear legitimate, but the actual domain is "accounts-center.com" — a domain controlled by the attacker.
5
Isolation tactic. "Do not share this email" is designed to prevent you from asking colleagues or IT for help. Legitimate security communications never ask you to keep them secret. This is a social engineering technique to isolate you from your support network.
Decision Point 2
The Follow-Up

Fifteen minutes later, you receive a phone call from someone claiming to be from the IT Help Desk. They say they're calling about the same security incident and need to "walk you through the verification process over the phone." They sound professional, use internal jargon, and know your department name.

They say: "Hi, this is David from the Service Desk. I'm calling regarding the credential verification we sent you earlier. I can see your account is flagged — I just need you to confirm your employee ID and current password so I can reset the flag on our end. It'll take 30 seconds."

What do you do?
A
Give them your employee ID and password — they sound legitimate and know your department.They're from IT, after all.
B
Hang up. Call the IT Help Desk directly using the number from the company intranet, not the number they called from.Verify through an independent channel.
C
Give them your employee ID but not your password, and ask them to verify themselves first.A compromise — partial information seems safer.
⚠ Vishing Attack Successful
This was a voice phishing (vishing) attack. The caller was not from IT. Attackers can easily find department names from LinkedIn or company websites. By sharing your password over the phone, you've given an attacker direct access to your account. Legitimate IT staff will never ask for your password.
✓ Perfect Response
You hung up and called IT independently — exactly right. By using a known, trusted number from the company intranet (not the one from the incoming call), you've verified the request through a separate channel. This is the "validate, validate, validate" principle in action. The real Help Desk confirms: there is no security incident. The email and the phone call were both part of the same coordinated attack.
⚡ Partial Compromise
You held back your password, which shows good instinct. But sharing your employee ID still gives the attacker a critical piece of information. Combined with other data (from LinkedIn, for example), an employee ID can be used for password reset attacks or to impersonate you in follow-up social engineering. The correct action is: share nothing, verify independently.
Decision Point 3
Reporting & Containment

You've identified the phishing email and the follow-up vishing call. Now you need to act. The phishing email is still sitting in your inbox. Other employees in your department may have received the same email.

What is the correct sequence of actions?
A
Delete the email, then send a warning to your team's group chat about the phishing attempt.Get rid of the evidence and warn others.
B
Use the "Report Phishing" button in Outlook to report the email to Cybersecurity. Then notify your manager. Do not delete the email.Follow the official reporting procedure.
C
Forward the email to Cybersecurity with a note explaining what happened, then delete it from your inbox.Give them the evidence and clean up.
⚠ Evidence Destroyed
By deleting the email, you've destroyed forensic evidence that the Cybersecurity team needs to analyze the attack, trace its origin, and protect other employees. Warning your team via group chat is well-intentioned but can cause panic and is not the proper escalation channel. The Cybersecurity team needs to control the response.
✓ Textbook Response
You used the official reporting mechanism, which automatically forwards the email headers and metadata to the Cybersecurity Incident Response team. You preserved the evidence, notified your manager (chain of command), and let the professionals handle the response. This is exactly the protocol that allows the team to quickly identify if others were targeted and block the attack domain firm-wide.
⚡ Close, But Not Quite
Forwarding the email is better than deleting it, but it doesn't capture the full email headers that the "Report Phishing" button preserves. Also, deleting after forwarding removes evidence from your mailbox that the forensic team may need to reference. The correct approach: use the built-in reporting tool and preserve the original.
Key Takeaway
The STOP Framework

Before you act on any email requesting credentials, transfers, or sensitive data — STOP and check these four things:

S
Sender
Check the actual email address — not the display name. Look for misspellings, unusual domains, or slight character substitutions.
T
Threat Language
Urgency, fear, and consequences ("your account will be suspended") are hallmarks of social engineering. Legitimate communications don't threaten you.
O
Odd Links
Hover over any link before clicking. Does the domain match the real company URL? If there's any doubt, navigate directly through your browser or bookmarks.
P
Pressure to Act
Tight deadlines, "don't tell anyone," requests to bypass normal processes — all pressure tactics. Slow down and verify through an independent channel.

EMAIL PHISHING
Mass emails impersonating IT, HR, or banks. Look for: spoofed domains, generic greetings, urgency, suspicious links. Most common attack vector — 90%+ of breaches start here.
BUSINESS EMAIL COMPROMISE (BEC)
Targeted attacks impersonating executives or vendors to authorize wire transfers. Caused $2.77 billion in U.S. losses in 2024. Look for: bypassed approval chains, secrecy requests, new payment instructions.
VISHING (VOICE PHISHING)
Phone calls impersonating IT, security, or executives. Surged 442% in 2024. Rule: never share passwords over the phone. Always hang up and call back via a known number.
COMPROMISED ACCOUNT
Attacks sent from a real colleague's hacked account. 58% of phishing emails now come from compromised legitimate accounts. Even internal messages can be threats — verify unusual requests in person or by phone.
